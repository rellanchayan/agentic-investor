## New Candidates

### 1. SPGI — S&P Global Inc.
**Sector:** Financials (data, analytics, and ratings)
**Exchange:** NYSE | Market cap: ~$128 billion | Recent price: ~$440

**The business.** S&P Global runs three interlocking franchises: the Ratings division (Moody's
and S&P together control ~80% of global credit ratings), the Market Intelligence division
(Bloomberg-alternative data for institutional clients), and the Indices division (the S&P 500
and thousands of derivative benchmarks whose licensing fees are essentially a toll on the entire
passive-investing industry). The 2022 merger with IHS Markit added commodities price data (Platts)
and financial workflow software.

**The moat.** Credit ratings are one of the purest regulated oligopolies in US finance. Bond
issuers must use an NRSRO-approved agency; only three NRSROs hold most of the market; and
changing to a new rater would destroy the trust of bond buyers. Switching costs are enormous on
both sides of the market. The S&P 500 index franchise is arguably even more durable — every
passive ETF and pension benchmark referencing "the S&P 500" pays SPGI a licensing fee, and no
competitor can create a substitute because the name itself IS the standard. Together these
generate recurring, high-margin revenue that grows without proportional cost increases.

**Why the price may be sensible now.** SPGI historically traded at 35-40x trailing earnings.
The current trailing P/E is approximately 27x and the forward P/E approximately 23x, a
meaningful compression from its typical range. GuruFocus GF Value places intrinsic value at
approximately $531 versus a recent price of ~$440 — a ~17% implied discount. Revenue guidance
for full-year 2026 is +6.6% to +8.6% with continued strong FCF and buybacks. This is not a
distressed stock; it is a high-quality compounder at a price that is unusually modest versus its
own history.

**Key risk.** A prolonged drought in debt issuance (a severe recession) hurts ratings revenue.
Regulatory risk — forced break-up of the ratings oligopoly — is always in the background but
has not materialized in 50 years. The IHS Markit integration cost synergy execution is still
in progress.

### 2. GE — GE Aerospace
**Sector:** Industrials (aviation propulsion)
**Exchange:** NYSE | Market cap: ~$370 billion (at ~$348/share) | Recent price: ~$348

**The business.** After spinning off GE Vernova (power/energy) in 2024 and GE HealthCare in
2023, the remaining General Electric was renamed GE Aerospace. It designs and manufactures jet
engines for commercial and military aircraft (LEAP engines for the 737 MAX and A320neo; GE9X
for the 787 Dreamliner; military engines including the F414). The company also provides
aftermarket MRO (maintenance, repair, overhaul) services throughout each engine's multi-decade
life. Total orders surged 87% year-over-year to $23 billion in 2026, and adjusted revenue grew
29% to $11.6 billion in the most recent period.

**The moat.** Once an airline buys a GE-powered aircraft, it must use GE-certified parts and
services for the engine's entire 20-30 year service life. Switching to another engine is
impossible mid-aircraft (it would mean replacing the plane). This aftermarket lock-in generates
recurring, high-margin revenue that is barely price-sensitive and not disrupted by new
entrants. GE Aerospace commands approximately 75% of the narrow-body jet engine market and 55%
of wide-body, with a reported backlog of $181 billion — roughly 7-8 years of current revenue.
Engine certification is a decade-long, multi-billion-dollar process; there are only two credible
Western competitors (GE and Pratt & Whitney/RTX), and entry is essentially closed.

**Why the price may be sensible now.** GE's 52-week range is $266-$389. The stock is up ~27%
year-on-year but is 10% off its 52-week high. Morningstar's published fair value estimate of
$307 (now likely stale given the 87% order growth and 29% revenue growth) may understate
the current business. The backlog provides earnings visibility that most industrial businesses
envy. The stock is not obviously cheap, but given the durability and scale of the aftermarket
moat, a research analyst should assess whether the current price implies a reasonable multiple
on normalized free cash flow.

**Key risk.** A major commercial aviation downturn (pandemic-style) reduces engine deliveries
and causes airlines to defer maintenance — the two main revenue streams. Supply-chain bottlenecks
(GE has publicly acknowledged delivery delays). Military budget cuts could affect defense revenue.
Single-aisle commercial aviation recovery is the key assumption.

### 3. WM — Waste Management Inc.
**Sector:** Industrials (environmental services)
**Exchange:** NYSE | Market cap: ~$85 billion | Recent price: ~$220 (estimated)

**The business.** Waste Management is the largest residential and commercial solid-waste
collection and disposal company in North America, operating roughly 260 active landfills,
hundreds of transfer stations, and a national fleet of collection trucks. It also operates
waste-to-energy plants and a growing recycling infrastructure business.

**The moat.** This is a deceptively wide-moat business. Permitted landfills cannot be replicated
— new landfill permits are almost never granted near population centers, making WM's existing
sites a permanent asset. Collection routes have strong route-density economics: once a truck
serves 60% of homes on a street, the marginal cost of adding the remaining 40% is near zero,
making it uneconomic for a smaller competitor to undercut WM in an established route. Long-term
municipal and commercial contracts (often 3-10 years) create sticky recurring revenue. WM raises
prices roughly in line with inflation — sometimes ahead of it — with minimal customer defection,
demonstrating real pricing power.

**Why the price may be sensible now.** WM guided for approximately $3.8 billion of free cash
flow in 2026. The trailing P/E of approximately 32x is elevated relative to the S&P 500 but
roughly in line with WM's own 10-year average for a business of this quality and
predictability. Simply Wall St's DCF analysis suggests approximately 8% undervaluation on cash
flow. The stock has lagged in 2026 on volume concerns, which may be a short-term issue rather
than a structural one. This is not a deeply discounted stock, but it is a consistent compounder
trading at a price that a disciplined investor should at least understand.

**Key risk.** Softer commercial waste volumes (economic slowdown reduces trash generated by
businesses). Regulatory changes to landfill methane/emissions rules could raise closure costs.
Recycling commodity prices are volatile and can swing from revenue to cost. The core collection
business is capital-intensive.

**MCD:** McDonald's franchise model generates ~50% operating margins with almost no capital on
the company's own balance sheet (franchisees own the restaurants). At $264.91, the P/E has
compressed to approximately 22-23x TTM, below the normal 25-35x range. Fair value estimates
from conservative analysts cluster around $330-$345. This is one of the most durable consumer
franchises in the world and appears to be trading at a genuine discount. Strong case for
thesis priority.

**PG:** Procter & Gamble's current P/E of 20.7x is 26% below its own 5-year historical average
of 24.6x. The stock yields approximately 3% with 70 consecutive years of dividend increases.
Revenue was $87 billion in FY2026 (+3.3% year-on-year). Analyst consensus target is $160.61
(+11.7% from current price). This is as clear a "quality Dividend Aristocrat at below-historical
valuation" signal as we are likely to see. Strong case for thesis priority.

**HD:** Home Depot dominates home improvement retail in North America with structural advantages
(professional contractor relationships, store density, supply-chain scale). The 10.9% 6-month
decline reflects housing market softness. The P/E of ~23x is roughly in line with HD's 10-year
average. GuruFocus GF Value of $378.86 implies ~14.8% undervaluation at the current $330
price. Merits a thesis, but is less urgent than MCD and PG given the smaller valuation gap and
the housing cycle risk.

**WMT:** Despite an 18.9% 6-month decline, Walmart's P/E of 36.6x is ABOVE its 10-year
average of 32x. GuruFocus GF Value of ~$103 is nearly exactly where the stock trades now. The
correction has not created a valuation opportunity; it has moved the stock from expensive to
roughly fair. Low priority for a thesis now.

**NKE:** The 35.1% decline is dramatic but the business is genuinely struggling: Greater China
revenues -11%, NIKE Direct -7%, Converse -32%. Some DCF-based fair value estimates put Nike at
$36.83 — below the current $39.59. This is a turnaround story with high uncertainty, not a
"quality at a fair price" opportunity. We should keep it on the watchlist and revisit if the
turnaround gains traction, but it is not a thesis priority today.
