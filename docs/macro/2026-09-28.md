## What each term means

- **SPY** is a fund that holds the 500 largest US companies. It is our benchmark — what we measure ourselves against over time.
- **200-day average** is the average price of SPY over the last ~200 trading days. When SPY is above this line, the market is generally in an uptrend. When it is below, a downtrend. Today SPY (764.58) is above its 200-day average (718.83), which is a mild positive.
- **Interest rates / the Fed** — the US Federal Reserve sets short-term interest rates. Higher rates make borrowing costlier and tend to push stock prices down by making bonds more attractive and raising the cost of capital for businesses.
- **Inflation (CPI)** — how fast prices are rising. Hot inflation can spook markets and push the Fed to raise rates further.
- **Earnings season** — the few weeks each quarter when companies report profits. The next major earnings wave begins mid-October.

## Why cautious, not normal?

Three things converge today:

1. **The market is near its most expensive level ever.** A Shiller CAPE of 40.6x sits at the 98.8th percentile of all months since 1881. The only time it was higher was late 1999, just before the dot-com crash. This does not mean a crash is imminent — expensive markets can stay expensive for years — but it means we are buying against a headwind. At these prices, the gap between "fair value" and "price" must be wide before we commit capital.

2. **Rates are high and rising.** The 10-year Treasury yields 5.17%, near its highest in 16 years. A bond paying 5% competes directly with stocks. The Fed funds rate is 3.88% and futures markets price in further hikes to 4.2% by December 2026. Rising rates are bad for high-valuation stocks in particular.

3. **Near-term macro uncertainty.** An oil spike this morning (Trump-Iran diplomatic breakdown, Brent above $107) raises inflation fears. The jobs report on Friday is the week's biggest wildcard — a hot number could push rate expectations higher and weigh on equities.

SPY is above its 200-day average, which means we are not in a bear-market crash. This is not a panic posture. It is simply a reminder that when the market is this expensive and rates are this high, our required margin of safety must be larger than normal.
