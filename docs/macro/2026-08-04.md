# Market Weather — 2026-08-04

## Posture: CAUTIOUS

**One-sentence reason:** The market is expensive by most historical measures, interest rates are elevated with a hawkish Fed, and two major data releases — a jobs report Friday and a CPI print next Wednesday — create meaningful near-term uncertainty, so we should demand a wider margin of safety than normal before deploying cash.

---

## What the numbers say

**SPY (our benchmark):** Closed yesterday at 765.68. Its 200-day moving average is 701.34. SPY is 9.2% above that average, which means the long-term trend is bullish — the market is in an uptrend, not a downtrend. That is the one genuinely positive signal today.

**Futures this morning:** S&P 500 futures were up about 0.21% before the open, a mild positive after Monday's strong 1.48% gain. The Nasdaq led Monday's rally with a 2.13% jump, driven by semiconductor and software stocks. Pre-market sentiment is calm and slightly positive.

**VIX (the "fear gauge"):** Opened at 16.82, prior close 15.99. The 52-week range has been 13.38 to 35.30. A reading of 17 is in the "normal, calm" zone — the market is not expressing fear or panic. There is no distressed-seller opportunity here; prices are not being thrown overboard.

**Market valuation — expensive:** The S&P 500 trailing price-to-earnings ratio is approximately 25.7x, against a long-run historical median of about 18x. The Shiller CAPE (which smooths earnings over ten years to remove one-year noise) is around 36.4x — roughly 76% above the modern-era average of 20.7x. By both measures, the overall market is priced for perfection, not for a margin of safety. A careful investor should be more demanding about price, not less.

**Interest rates — elevated:** The Federal Reserve met on July 28-29 and held the fed funds rate at 3.50%–3.75%, but three of twelve voting members dissented in favor of raising rates further. This is a hawkish signal: the Fed is not cutting, and a minority wants to tighten more. The 10-year US Treasury yield is ~4.69%. When risk-free bonds pay 4.7%, stocks need to offer an even better return to justify their risk — which pushes the required margin of safety upward for any stock purchase.

---

## Earnings season context

We are mid-way through Q2 2026 earnings season. About 61% of S&P 500 companies have reported; 86% have beaten estimates. Year-over-year earnings growth is running at approximately 29%. That sounds great, and the fundamentals of many businesses are strong. However, good earnings are now widely expected and are already priced in at a 25-29x P/E multiple. A beat that matches expectations rarely produces a big pop; the market moves on surprises. Tech and AI capital spending is drawing scrutiny ("capex vigilantes") even as revenues beat.

---

## What this means for us today

Our three candidates with active theses are all priced well above our fair-value estimates:

- AAPL at $305.75 vs our base fair value of $195 — trades at a 56.8% premium to what we'd willingly pay.
- MSFT at $496.63 vs our base fair value of $378 — trades at a 31.4% premium.
- V at $356.07 vs our base fair value of $297 — trades at a 19.9% premium.

Under any posture — cautious, normal, or opportunistic — none of these three would qualify for a buy today. The cautious posture does not change today's expected outcome (hold cash), but it frames how the portfolio manager should evaluate anything new: demand a bigger discount, not a smaller one.

We are also returning from a seven-day gap in morning reviews. The playbook lesson from 2026-08-03 explicitly requires us to re-verify the underlying business assumptions in each thesis before using its fair-value estimate to set an entry price. A price check alone is not enough after an 11-day gap. The portfolio manager should treat the thesis fair values as potentially stale until re-verified against recent filings or earnings.

**Bottom line:** Hold cash. The market is not on sale. Two data events this week add uncertainty. When and if a quality business trades at a genuine margin of safety, we will be ready with full cash. That patience is the plan, not a failure.
