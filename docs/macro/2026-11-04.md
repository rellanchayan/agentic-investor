## Interest rates and the Fed

The **Federal Reserve** (the US central bank) met on October 27-28 and **held its benchmark interest rate unchanged at 3.75%-4.00%**. That decision passed 10-2: two voting members (Governor Cook and President Hammack) dissented because they wanted an immediate hike. That is a hawkish signal — it means the people setting rates are, if anything, leaning toward higher rates, not lower.

The Fed **raised rates in September** (the first hike of this cycle), held in October, and markets are now pricing roughly an **85% chance of another hike at the December 9 meeting** (which would take rates to 4.00%-4.25%).

Why does this matter? Higher interest rates make borrowing more expensive for companies and consumers. They also make bonds more attractive relative to stocks, which puts downward pressure on stock valuations. Rising rates are a headwind for equities, especially expensive ones.

## Inflation (CPI)

The most recent inflation reading — September 2026 — showed consumer prices rising **+3.7% from a year earlier**, up from +3.4% in August. The Fed's target is 2%. Inflation re-accelerating away from the target gives the Fed reason to keep raising rates.

The **next CPI report** (covering October 2026) is due **Tuesday, November 10 at 8:30am ET** — just one week away. If it comes in hot, it will cement December rate hike expectations. If it surprises lower, it may ease some pressure.

## Are stocks expensive?

Yes. By the available measures:

- **Trailing P/E** (price divided by the past 12 months of earnings): around **29-30** as of August 2026, well above the long-run average of 16-19.
- **Forward P/E** (price divided by forecast next-year earnings): around **21-22x**, above the 10-year average of roughly 18.8x.
- **Shiller CAPE** (a smoothed, inflation-adjusted P/E): around 37-39 earlier in 2026, near the second-highest reading ever recorded.

This does not mean stocks must fall immediately — expensive markets can stay expensive for a long time. But it does mean there is less cushion if earnings disappoint or rates rise faster than expected. And it means we need a bigger-than-normal discount to find genuine value.

That is consistent with what we see in our own candidate list: every thesis we have shows current prices 35-50% above our base fair-value estimates. The market's expensiveness is showing up in our own numbers.

The cautious posture does not change the thesis-writing obligation; it sharpens the discount we will demand when prices do fall.
