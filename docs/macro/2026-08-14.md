# Market Weather — 2026-08-14

## Posture: CAUTIOUS

**One-sentence reason:** The S&P 500 is at its 25th all-time high of 2026, valuations are well above historical norms, the Fed's rate is still restrictive with a live hike risk for September, and next week brings two policy-sensitive events (FOMC Minutes, Jackson Hole) that could shift the whole market's direction.

---

## What the numbers say

**SPY vs its 200-day average**

SPY is priced at $777.26 this morning. Its 200-day average — the average closing price over the past ~200 trading days — is $705.43. That puts SPY about 10.2% above the average, confirming a clear long-term uptrend.

Being above the 200-day average is reassuring: the big trend has not broken. But a 10% cushion above a rising average also tells us the market has already done a lot of running. It did not get cheap while we held cash.

**Futures and market direction this morning (Aug 14)**

S&P 500 futures are mixed this morning after the index closed at a record high on Thursday, clearing the 7,800 level for the first time in its history. The S&P 500 gained about 0.1% early in Friday's session; the Nasdaq was up about 0.2%. Markets are calm but slightly risk-on. This is the 25th record close the S&P 500 has notched in 2026 alone.

The mood is being balanced against incoming retail sales data (see Events below) and ongoing Middle East geopolitical tensions, which have kept some traders cautious.

**Valuation — is the market cheap or expensive?**

The S&P 500's trailing price-to-earnings ratio (what investors are paying today for the past year's earnings) sits around 26-29x. The long-run historical median is about 18x. In plain English: investors are paying roughly 50% more per dollar of earnings than the historical average.

The forward P/E — based on next year's estimated earnings — is around 20-21x, which is also above its own historical median of about 18-20x. The Shiller CAPE (a smoothed, inflation-adjusted version of the P/E ratio) sits near 39x, one of the highest readings outside the late 1990s dot-com era.

This does not mean a crash is coming. Markets can stay expensive for a long time. But it does mean there is little room for error: if earnings disappoint or rates rise further, stocks have room to fall without becoming "cheap."

**Interest rates and the Fed**

The Federal Reserve held its benchmark interest rate at 3.50%-3.75% at its July 29 meeting, unchanged for the fifth consecutive meeting. Three Fed officials voted to raise rates further.

The next scheduled Fed decision is September 16. Markets currently imply a 38-42% probability that the Fed raises rates at that meeting — a meaningful chance, not a remote one. There is essentially zero probability priced for a rate cut.

Why rates matter: a higher interest rate makes it more expensive for companies to borrow and invest, and it means the US government's risk-free bonds pay a decent return. When 10-year Treasury bonds yield ~4.69%, investors have a real alternative to owning stocks. That competes with stock valuations and puts a ceiling on how much investors are willing to pay.

**Inflation**

The July Consumer Price Index (CPI), released on August 12, showed prices rising 3.4% over the past year — down slightly from 3.5% in June. Monthly prices rose just 0.1%, and core inflation (excluding food and energy) came in at 2.5% annually. Both readings were in-line with expectations.

The market took this well: the S&P 500 gained about 0.5% on the release day. But 3.4% annual inflation is still well above the Fed's 2% target. The Fed has been fighting inflation for more than five years; this is progress, not victory.

Also on Thursday (August 13): the Producer Price Index (PPI) — wholesale prices — came in flat for July, with the annual rate slowing sharply from 5.5% to 4.7%. That is genuinely good news: cheaper inputs for businesses reduce future price pressure on consumers. Futures got a mild lift from the PPI print.

**The ongoing wildcard: the July jobs shock**

On August 7, the government reported the US economy shed 23,000 jobs in July. Economists had expected a gain of 80,000-95,000. That is a miss of over 100,000 jobs. Prior months were also revised down by 103,000 combined.

This creates a difficult situation for the Fed: inflation is still too high (argues for raising rates or holding firm), while the job market just unexpectedly contracted (argues for cutting rates or holding firm). This tension is called stagflation risk — slow growth combined with sticky prices. It is the scenario the Fed most dreads, and it explains why markets only price a 38-42% chance of a September hike rather than near-certainty.

## What this means for us today

Hold all cash. No buys are possible.

The cautious posture is the right call for the following reasons:

1. The market is at record highs with above-average valuations. The P/E ratio of ~26-29x is about 50% higher than the long-run median, leaving limited room for earnings disappointments.
2. The Fed is still restrictive. Rates at 3.50-3.75% with a real chance of going higher in September means the cost of capital is elevated. The 10-year yield near 4.69% provides a meaningful competing alternative to stocks.
3. Stagflation risk is real. The July jobs shock and still-elevated inflation put the Fed in a bind it cannot easily escape. Uncertainty about the Fed's path is itself a reason for caution.
4. Next week brings two major policy events. The FOMC Minutes (Aug 20) and Jackson Hole (Aug 21) can move the whole market. We do not want to buy ahead of an event that could shift rate expectations sharply in either direction.
5. Our candidates are not on sale regardless. Even if the macro picture were perfect, AAPL, MSFT, and V are all too expensive against our fair values to buy today.

The 200-day trend is still intact — the structural picture has not broken. We are being patient, not panicking. Cash in a 3.50-3.75% rate environment earns something. We hold it without apology until the market or a name gives us the margin of safety we require.
