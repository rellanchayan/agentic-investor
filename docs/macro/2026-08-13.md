# Market Weather — 2026-08-13

## Posture: CAUTIOUS

**One-sentence reason:** The market trades at a meaningful premium to historical P/E averages, inflation has been stuck above the Fed's target for over five years, and last week's shocking jobs contraction creates a stagflationary backdrop that warrants demanding a bigger discount before buying anything.

---

## What the numbers say

**SPY vs its 200-day average**

SPY closed Tuesday at $778.37. Its 200-day average (the average closing price over the past ~200 trading days) is $704.97. That puts SPY about 10.4% above the average — a meaningful uptrend, not a whisker above the line. The long-term structural trend is up and has been for some time.

Being above the 200-day average is a good sign for trend direction, but it also means the market did not get cheap. It has been rising while we held cash.

**Futures this morning (Aug 13)**

S&P 500 futures are up roughly 0.2% and Nasdaq futures are up roughly 0.6% heading into today's open. Markets got a modest boost this morning from July PPI coming in cooler than expected (see below). The open should be a quiet, slightly positive one barring any surprise in jobless claims.

**Valuation (how expensive is the market?)**

The S&P 500's trailing price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio is approximately 25-26x earnings. The 20-year historical average is around 17x. The forward P/E (based on next twelve months' estimated earnings) is about 20x, which is also above its own historical norm. In plain English: the market is not on sale. You are paying a premium for a dollar of earnings compared to most of history. This is not a crisis, but it does mean the margin for error if earnings disappoint is thin.

**Interest rates and the Fed**

The Federal Reserve held its benchmark interest rate at 3.50%-3.75% at its most recent meeting on July 29, 2026. The vote was 9-3, with three regional Fed presidents dissenting — they wanted to raise rates further because inflation has remained above the Fed's 2% target for more than five years. The next FOMC meeting is September 16, 2026.

Markets currently price a 67% chance the Fed holds steady in September, but a meaningful 33% chance of a further rate hike. There is almost no probability priced for a cut.

Why does this matter? Higher rates make future company profits worth less in today's dollars. When rates are elevated and there is a real chance they go higher, that puts pressure on stock valuations — particularly for expensive growth stocks.

## The two key data points this week

**July CPI — released yesterday, August 12**

The Consumer Price Index rose 0.1% in July (monthly) and 3.4% over the past year. Core CPI (stripping out food and energy) rose 0.2% monthly. This was roughly in-line with expectations — not hot, not cold. Markets took it well: the S&P 500 gained about 0.5% and the Nasdaq gained about 0.9% on Tuesday. However, 3.4% annual inflation is still well above the Fed's 2% target, and the Fed has been fighting this for over five years. Relief, yes — but not victory.

**July PPI — released this morning, August 13**

The Producer Price Index (a measure of wholesale prices that often feeds into future consumer prices) came in flat for July, with the annual rate decelerating to 4.7% from 5.5% in June — more than analysts expected. This is genuinely good news for the inflation picture: cheaper inputs for businesses means less pressure to raise prices for consumers down the road. Futures got a modest lift on this print.

## The wildcard: the July jobs shock

On August 7, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported that the US economy unexpectedly shed 23,000 jobs in July. Wall Street had forecast a gain of 110,000. That is a 133,000-job miss — not a rounding error.

Making it worse: revisions knocked 103,000 previously reported jobs off the May and June figures. So the labor market was already weaker than the official numbers suggested, and July made it worse.

Government and education lost over 50,000 jobs. Leisure and hospitality fell 40,000. Wage growth slipped to 3.2% annually — its slowest pace since 2021.

What does this mean? The US economy is now sending two conflicting signals at once: inflation that is too high for comfort (3.4%), and a labor market that just contracted unexpectedly. This combination is called stagflation risk — slow growth plus sticky prices. It is the scenario the Fed most dreads, because cutting rates to help jobs would worsen inflation, while raising rates to fight inflation would worsen jobs. The Fed is in a bind.

## What this means for us today

Hold all cash. No buys are possible — our three thesis candidates are too expensive against our fair-value estimates regardless of posture.

The cautious posture is the right call because:

1. The market is expensive relative to history (P/E ~25-26x vs 20-year average ~17x).
2. Inflation remains stubbornly above the Fed's target, and the Fed could raise rates again in September.
3. The July jobs shock (-23,000 vs +110,000 expected) introduces genuine economic uncertainty. A soft labor market plus sticky inflation is the most uncomfortable environment for the Fed to navigate.
4. Retail sales data on Friday is a real risk event. A miss there could accelerate concerns about economic slowdown.

The 200-day trend is still up — the structural picture has not broken. We are being patient, not panicking. When our thesis names eventually trade at or below fair value, we will be ready to act. Until then, cash earns more in this rate environment than a forced buy above fair value.
