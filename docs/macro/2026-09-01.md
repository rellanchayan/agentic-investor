## What each term means (for reference)

- **SPY**: the fund that holds the 500 largest US companies. It is our benchmark — "the market."
- **200-day average**: the average price of SPY over the last ~200 trading days. Price above it = uptrend; below it = downtrend.
- **PE ratio**: price divided by earnings. Higher = more expensive relative to profits.
- **Shiller CAPE**: same idea but smoothed over 10 years of inflation-adjusted earnings. A longer-term read on whether the market is cheap or dear.
- **10-year Treasury yield**: the interest rate the US government pays to borrow for 10 years. When yields are high, safe government bonds compete with stocks for investor money.
- **FOMC / the Fed**: the Federal Reserve sets short-term interest rates. Its next decision is September 16.

### Valuation — is the market cheap or expensive?
The market looks **expensive** by most standard measures:

- **Trailing PE ratio: ~29.7** — the historical long-run average is around 16–19. We are significantly above that.
- **Shiller CAPE (10-year smoothed PE): ~42.2** — this is near the upper end of its historical range, a level associated with future returns being below average.
- **Forward PE: ~20.0** — above both the 5-year average (19.9) and the 10-year average (19.0).

In plain terms: you are paying roughly $30 for every $1 of current earnings, versus a historical norm of about $17–19. The market is not catastrophically overvalued, but it is not cheap either.

### Interest rates
- **Fed funds rate: 3.50–3.75%** (current target range, unchanged at last meeting).
- **10-year Treasury yield: 4.79%** as of this morning, up a few basis points overnight on oil/inflation concerns.

A 4.79% risk-free yield from a government bond is meaningful competition for stocks. Historically, when the 10-year yield is this high, stocks need to offer a better expected return to justify the extra risk — which means prices need to be lower (or earnings need to grow fast).
