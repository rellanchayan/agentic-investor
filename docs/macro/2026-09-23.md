# Market Weather — Wednesday, September 23, 2026

## Posture: CAUTIOUS

**One-sentence reason:** The market is near dot-com-bubble valuation extremes (CAPE ~40.6x, the second-highest reading in 145 years of data) while the Fed has just begun raising rates again — two conditions that, taken together, require a meaningfully larger discount before any new buy makes sense.

---

## What the numbers say

**SPY (our benchmark):** $769.21, which is **+7.2% above its 200-day moving average** of $717.58. A price above the 200-day average means the market is in a general uptrend — prices are higher on average than they were over the past year. This is not a beaten-down market where fear is driving a fire sale.

**Valuation (CAPE ratio ~40.6x):** The Shiller CAPE is a measure of how expensive the S&P 500 is relative to long-term earnings. The historical average is about 17x. At 40.6x we are at the 98.8th percentile of all months since 1881 — meaning the market has been cheaper than this 98.8% of the time in recorded history. The only period more expensive was the dot-com bubble peak of 1999-2000. High CAPE does not predict the timing of a decline, but it does mean the price you pay for a dollar of future earnings is historically very steep.

**Interest rates:** The Federal Reserve raised its benchmark rate by 0.25 percentage points on September 16, 2026 — the first rate hike in over three years — to a range of 3.75%–4.00%. The 10-year US Treasury bond now yields roughly 4.96%. Higher rates mean: (a) borrowing costs more for companies and consumers, which can slow earnings growth; and (b) a "safe" bond now pays nearly 5% per year, creating real competition for stocks that only earn 2-3% in dividends or buybacks.

**Futures this morning:** S&P 500 futures are up a slight +0.11%, Nasdaq +0.06%, Dow +0.16%. The open is pointing mildly positive, not a dramatic move in either direction.

## What this means for us today

We are long-term investors who buy good companies at a fair price and hold for years. The market's extreme valuation level (CAPE ~40.6x) and rising interest rates mean we should demand a **larger-than-normal margin of safety** before placing any buy. Our normal threshold is 12%; under a cautious posture, we require at least 18% margin of safety (a 6-percentage-point premium) on any name we consider.

In practice this changes nothing today: our three thesis candidates with current valuations (AAPL, MSFT, V) are already 24-46% above our entry prices — far beyond even the cautious bar. But the posture matters for the portfolio manager's mindset: in a frothy market, we do not shave margins of safety to make a name "fit," we wait.

The account is 100% cash ($1,000,000). There is no urgency to deploy capital into an expensive market. Patient inaction in an expensive market is not failure — it is discipline.
