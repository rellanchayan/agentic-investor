## Interest rates and the Fed

The Federal Reserve raised short-term rates by **25 basis points on September 16, 2026** —
its first hike in over three years — in a unanimous 12-0 vote. The Fed is back in
"fighting inflation" mode.

The **10-year US Treasury yield crossed above 5%** (as of September 29), which is noteworthy
because it exceeds the S&P 500's earnings yield for the first time in roughly 25 years. In
plain terms: bonds now pay more than stocks are earning, which makes stocks relatively less
attractive on a pure income comparison.

Rising rates hurt stock prices because:
1. Borrowing costs for companies go up, squeezing profits.
2. Risk-free bonds become a real alternative to equities.
3. Discount rates used in valuation models rise, which mechanically lowers fair-value estimates.

## Is the market expensive or cheap?

Very expensive by historical standards.

The **Shiller CAPE ratio** (a measure of how expensive the S&P 500 is relative to average
earnings over the past 10 years, adjusted for inflation) is **~40.6x** as of September 2026.
That puts it at the **98.8th percentile** of all readings since 1881 — meaning stocks have
been this expensive or more expensive only about 1.2% of all months in 145 years of history.
The last comparable CAPE reading preceded a ~49% market decline.

The "Excess CAPE Yield" (roughly: how much more stocks earn than bonds on a long-run basis)
is now only about **1%** — very thin. Yale economist Robert Shiller suggests this implies
stocks may only beat bonds by about 1% per year over the next decade at these prices.

**None of this predicts a crash tomorrow.** Expensive markets can stay expensive for years.
But it does mean: if we buy at today's prices, the margin for error is thin. The right
response is to demand a larger discount — i.e., to hold out for a better price — not to panic.
