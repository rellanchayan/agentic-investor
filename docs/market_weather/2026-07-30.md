## What the numbers say

### SPY vs its 200-day average

SPY is at **$738.00**. Its 200-day average — the average closing price across the last ~200 trading days, a rough line between "uptrend" and "downtrend" — is **$699.92**. SPY is 5.4% above that line.

Being above the 200-day average is a positive structural signal. The long-term trend is up. But it also means the market did not hand us a sale; it ran further away from us.

### Futures this morning

S&P 500 futures are pointing **up about 0.7%** as of early morning. The catalyst is Microsoft's fiscal Q4 earnings (reported last night after the bell), which beat on every line: revenue of $90 billion against an $87.4 billion estimate, Azure cloud growth of 43%, and EPS of $4.81 against a $4.21 estimate. MSFT stock jumped roughly 8% in premarket trading. Earnings season is active and broadly supportive of the market this week.

### The Fed decision (yesterday, July 29)

The Federal Open Market Committee met July 28-29 and voted 9-3 to **hold the federal funds rate steady at 3.50%-3.75%** — the fifth consecutive meeting with no change. This was not a surprise.

What matters more than the hold is the **three dissents**: Beth Hammack, Neel Kashkari, and Lorie Logan all voted to raise rates by another 0.25%. Three out of twelve committee members wanting an immediate hike is a clear hawkish signal. Markets are now pricing in two 25-basis-point rate hikes before year-end 2026. Higher expected rates compress what investors are willing to pay for future earnings — a headwind for valuations.

### Interest rates

The 10-year Treasury yield is approximately **4.70%** today, up from roughly 4.64%-4.68% on July 23. Rates are drifting higher, not lower. For a long-term investor, higher yields mean two things: (a) bonds are offering a more competitive return versus stocks, and (b) the discount rate used to value future cash flows is rising, which pushes theoretical fair values down.

### Inflation (today's data, released at 8:30am ET)

The Bureau of Economic Analysis released the June Personal Consumption Expenditures (PCE) price index — the Federal Reserve's preferred inflation gauge — this morning:

- **Headline PCE:** +3.7% year-over-year (-0.1% month-over-month). In line with expectations, and down from 4.1% in May. Progress.
- **Core PCE (ex-food and energy):** +3.3% year-over-year (+0.1% month-over-month). Slightly cooler than the expected +0.2% monthly reading. Also progress.

The direction is right — inflation is gradually easing. But 3.3% is still **65% above the Fed's 2% target**. The job is not done, which is why three Fed members wanted a hike yesterday and why the market expects more hikes ahead.

### GDP (today's data, released at 8:30am ET)

The Bureau of Economic Analysis also released the advance estimate for Q2 2026 real GDP growth: **+1.5% annualized**, slowing from +2.1% in Q1 2026. Consumer spending and investment are still growing, but government spending declined. The economy is expanding, but it is decelerating. Slower growth alongside still-elevated inflation is a mild stagflationary signal — the kind of environment where the Fed has less room to cut rates to support stocks.

### Labor market (today's data, released at 8:30am ET)

Initial weekly jobless claims came in at **197,000**, modestly below the 201,000 forecast. Continuing claims also fell. The labor market remains healthy — employers are neither hiring aggressively nor laying off. This is broadly good news: no imminent recession signal here. But a tight labor market also keeps wage pressure alive, which feeds into services inflation, which is part of why core PCE remains elevated.

### Valuation

The S&P 500 forward price-to-earnings ratio is approximately **19.82x** — just below 20x for the first time in several weeks. This represents some improvement from the 20.3x level noted in the July 23 review. It sits roughly at the 5-year average, meaning the market is neither screaming "expensive" nor "cheap" relative to recent history. But historical context matters: 19.82x is above the long-run median of roughly 18x, and in a rising-rate environment, this multiple is harder to justify than it would be when rates are near zero.

### Oil and geopolitics

Brent crude is approximately **$90 per barrel**, down from the ~$98 peak on July 23 but still elevated relative to the $78-80 range seen in early July. The US-Iran situation remains active: US forces have struck IRGC sites, Iran retaliated with an attack on a US military base in Jordan, and shipping in the Persian Gulf region is under threat. Oil markets remain volatile. At $90/bbl, energy costs are a persistent inflation input. The risk of a further spike has not disappeared.

## What this means for us today

**Hold all cash. No orders.**

The market's technical trend has improved — SPY reclaimed its 200-day average, earnings season is strong, and the FOMC meeting is now in the rearview mirror. These are genuine positives that push toward a less cautious stance over time. But the fundamental backdrop has not cleared enough to justify relaxing the posture today:

- Inflation is easing but remains 1.65 times the Fed's target.
- Three Fed members explicitly wanted a hike yesterday; markets expect two more this year.
- Yields are rising, not falling — a headwind for valuations.
- Every name on our watchlist is priced for near-perfection, not at a discount.
- Geopolitical risk (US-Iran, elevated oil) is still live.

Cautious posture is not pessimism — it is the correct response to a market where good companies are priced well above what we believe they are worth. We do not lower the bar to make a trade happen. We wait until prices come to us, or until a compelling new opportunity emerges at an appropriate discount.

If the MSFT earnings result leads to a thesis refresh that materially raises the fair value estimate, that could be worth examining in the coming days. But today's weather calls for patience.
